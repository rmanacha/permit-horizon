## 5. Observations (written 2026-11-03, Phase 3 sanity review)

1. **Scale.** Nationally, fees (+4.5% of baseline) and missing middle (+4.3%) add the most at mid, then ADUs (+3.8%), parking (+1.0%) and TOD (+0.1%). The combined package adds 13.6%. The largest single-state combined share, Rhode Island's 33.7%, is the smallest baseline (7,773 permits) set against a large central-county single-family stock.
2. **"% of baseline" reflects the denominator.** Missing middle and ADUs scale with the existing detached stock, the baseline with recent building. Slow-building states with large central counties read high (RI 18.3%, MA 13.5%, CT 12.8%, IL 11.3% for missing middle); fast-building Sun Belt states read low (NC 1.9%, SC 2.0%, TX 2.3%) even though their absolute increments are large (TX 25,066). The front-end should show permits first and the percentage second.
3. **ADU increments are overstated wherever a statewide ADU law already exists.** Only New Hampshire has a current-law file (strength 0.75, so its 1.1% is the lowest in the country). California (10.7%), Massachusetts (13.5%), Oregon, Washington, Montana, Vermont, Maine and others have statewide ADU laws, but they default to strength 0 until Phase 5 files their records. This is the largest known bias in the results, and it runs upward.
4. **California consistency check.** Our California ADU benchmark, 17,352 completed ADUs a year, sits at the low end of California's actual 17,500 to 22,500 a year (2023-2025, de-duplicated). It should match, since the tiers were fitted on California cities; it runs slightly low because Los Angeles is left out of the fit.
5. **Fees: a pure price effect.** The same $25,000 cut is 2.0% of the result in Hawaii and 7.3% in Mississippi, because $25,000 is a smaller share of an expensive home. Actual fee levels by state are not modeled (half of NAHB's national $13,627 is the variant); where fees are low the challenge premise overstates the cut.
6. **Parking follows apartment building.** The response applies to 5+ unit permits, so it is largest in DC (1.85%; 89% of DC's baseline is apartments, all in a central county) and smallest where few apartments are built (Delaware, Mississippi, Wyoming: 5+ is 6-13% of the baseline) or homes are very expensive (Hawaii: the saving is 1% of the price).
7. **TOD is near zero almost everywhere.** It is zero in 15 states with no rail, streetcar or ferry stop near housing, largest in DC (1.2%), New York (2,246 permits) and California (1,042), and 5,793 nationally. That is consistent with Kestelman (applications up, supply not) and Freemark (no permit effect in Chicago).
8. **Data bug found: Connecticut has no block groups.** The 2020 centers of population use Connecticut's legacy county codes (09001...), and the 2025 Gazetteer uses the nine planning regions (09110...), so the build dropped all 2,716 block groups. Connecticut's TOD (0) and its combined-package TOD rule are therefore wrong, despite Metro-North and Hartford Line service; everything else uses county data and is unaffected. Fixing it needs a 2020-tract to planning-region crosswalk in the Phase 1 pipeline (proposed as a follow-up task). No other state dropped a block group.
   **Fixed 2026-11-03 (commit 24e610a).** The Census relationship file `acs22_cousub22_blkgrp20_st09.txt` recodes each 2020 block group to its planning region; all 2,716 are kept and all match an ACS 2020-2024 row. 93 block groups (61,965 homes, 4.0% of the state's) lie within half a mile of a major stop, against 3.3% in Rhode Island. CT TOD now reads 0 / 34 / 391 permits (low / mid / high; benchmark 36). The combined package's transit-first rule now moves 17,049 detached homes from missing middle to TOD at mid, so the package falls from 8,634 to 8,568 (26.4% of baseline): missing middle loses 101 permits and TOD adds 34. National TOD is 5,827 and the zero-TOD count in observation 7 is now 14 states. **Limit:** the National Transit Map has no CTrail Hartford Line or Shore Line East feed (only Metro-North and Long Island Rail Road rail stops fall in Connecticut, and Amtrak is not in it), so all near-stop block groups are on the New Haven Line and its branches (Western 55, South Central 15, Naugatuck Valley 15, Greater Bridgeport 8) and Hartford, Meriden, New London and Old Saybrook read as far from rail. CT TOD is understated for that reason.
9. **Upper bounds hold.** The largest missing-middle plus TOD high case is 0.71% of a state's housing stock (California), far below Auckland's 4.1% in five years after upzoning three-quarters of its residential land. No result is implausibly large.
10. **One method fix came out of this review.** Comparing New Hampshire with Adam's v2.0 showed our high set equaled our joint favorable case (parking 2,477 against his 400), because every parameter moved together. The low and high sets now move only each scenario's evidence range (decision 72), and NH reads parking 0 / 157 / 596 against Adam's 0 / 100 / 400. Each remaining difference from Adam is explained in one sentence in the Phase 3 report. No formula bug was found in the hand checks.
