# Policy Scenario Comparison

If your state passed one of five housing reforms, how many more homes would get building permits over 2026-2030 than under the laws you have now? This page compares the five in Utah, New Hampshire and Arizona. Every table is generated from Permit Horizon's published results.

**How to read the numbers.** The figure to compare is the *increment over current law*: extra building permits over five years, after subtracting what your state's statutes already allow. The middle number uses the middle assumptions. The low and high numbers use the low and high assumption sets; they show how far honest disagreement about the evidence moves the answer, and no probability is attached to them.

# Scenarios Addressed

All five benchmark reforms, each against a no-change baseline: **ADUs** (one accessory dwelling unit by right on every single-family lot), **missing middle** (two to six homes on lots now limited to one house), **transit-oriented density** (more homes allowed near rail, streetcar, subway and ferry stops), **no parking minimums** for new homes, and **fees cut in half** (development and impact fees from $50,000 a home to $25,000).

We picked three states that differ in the ways that matter. Utah is growing fast, has a partial statewide ADU law, and publishes parcel data. New Hampshire is small and slow-growing, has little rail and thin local data, and already has a strong ADU law and a 2025 cap on parking. Arizona is a fast-growing Sun Belt state with a new ADU law that reaches 80% of its homes and a middle-housing law limited to areas near downtowns. The website has the same comparison for every state and DC.

# What Drives the Differences

**Fees come out on top in Utah and Arizona because they touch every home.** A fee cut lowers the cost of every permit in the baseline, houses and apartments alike, and $25,000 is a bigger share of a new home where homes cost less. But the whole estimate rests on one number, how strongly builders respond to cost, and the low end of that evidence is no response at all. So fees have the widest range of the five and start at zero.

**Missing middle is large wherever many single-family homes sit in the central county of a metro.** The estimate counts detached homes in central metropolitan counties and applies Portland's first-year rate. New Hampshire's two central counties hold almost half its detached homes, which is why missing middle ranks first there. Arizona already has a middle-housing law, but it reaches only land near the downtowns of its largest cities (about 5% of homes), so 96% of the reform would still be new.

**ADUs rank low because these three states already have most of the reform.** This is the dimmer switch. Arizona's ADU benchmark is about 12,600 completed ADUs, but Arizona's law already gives 80% of it, so only a fifth is new. New Hampshire's law gives 75% and Utah's 50%. A state with no ADU law would see the whole benchmark.

**Parking counts apartments only.** Removing a parking minimum saves the cost of spaces builders would otherwise skip, which matters most for buildings of five or more homes in metro cores. New Hampshire's 2025 cap of one space per home has already done 43% of it.

**Transit-oriented density is near zero in all three.** The rate comes from Los Angeles, where applications rose far more than new supply, so it is small; in Chicago permits didn't rise at all. New Hampshire also has very little housing near rail. Bus rapid transit isn't counted, because the national stop map can't identify it.

# Which Assumptions Matter Most

**One number flips the top of every ranking: the builder response.** It is the share by which five years of building rises when the cost of a new home falls 1%. At zero, the parking and fee estimates vanish and missing middle comes first in all three states. At its high end (2.84%), fees lead everywhere. The middle (0.75%) comes from one study of construction and prices; the evidence doesn't settle it, and you should say so out loud when quoting a fee or parking number.

**Missing middle rests on one city's first year.** Set the rate to zero (the low set's stress case) and missing middle drops to last; at Portland's gross ceiling it leads in all three states.

**Current law is the third lever, and the easiest one for a legislator to check.** The increment scales with the share of the reform not yet in law, so a wrong reading of a statute moves the answer one for one. If Utah's ADU law covered nothing, its ADU increment would double. Each statute reading is in the state's file with a quote and link; a team member's sign-off is still pending for these three states.

# What the Tool Cannot Say

- **Which towns, neighborhoods or lots would build.** The results are statewide.
- **Whether homes get cheaper, or who lives in them.** The tool counts permits. It doesn't estimate prices, rents, affordability, displacement or homelessness.
- **How likely any number is.** Low, middle and high are named assumption sets. Nobody can say the middle is twice as likely as the low.
- **What happens in a downturn.** The baseline comes from history; in the 2005-2010 bust only 56% of real five-year totals landed inside its range.
- **How towns will implement a state law.** Local fees, permitting speed, discretionary review and opposition can slow a reform that looks strong on paper. The tool reads state law only.
- **What happens after 2030**, or when several reforms pass together. The combined figure removes the overlaps we can name and is an illustration.
- **A difference of a few dozen permits.** That is inside the noise of the inputs.
